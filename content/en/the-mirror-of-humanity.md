## The iceman and the town crier

Imagine you are an iceman. It is 1880. Every winter you go to the frozen lake, cut blocks with a saw and store them under sawdust. In summer you deliver them by cart, house by house. Your father did the same. Your trade is necessary. Without it, food goes bad.

Now someone tells you that in a few decades ice will no longer be a business. That every house will have a box that makes it by itself, with something you cannot see that travels along a wire. You would not understand. It would not seem like a threat. It would seem like a joke.

Now imagine you are the town crier. You walk the streets announcing proclamations, news and prices. That is how people find things out. Your trade is as old as cities, and people trust you. Someone tells you that soon there will be a box that speaks with the voice of a man who is not in the room. And that many people, in different places, will hear it at the same time. You would probably answer that ghosts do not exist. Because in those days, a voice without a body could only be a ghost. And a voice without a body that sounds everywhere at once is not even a ghost. It is something omnipresent, almost a god. Madness.

Look at what happened. Centuries ago, someone could easily have said: "If one day we master lightning, we will be able to light up the world at night." It would have sounded like a dream, like science fiction. But it could be imagined, because the spark already gave light and lightning already lit up the whole sky. Now try to take the next step with his mind. From lightning to ice in the middle of summer. From lightning to a voice sounding in an empty room. Today it seems a simple step, because we have already seen it taken. For him it was impossible. He did not lack imagination. He lacked the ground to rest it on.

And what came next would have sounded even more absurd. In 1880, a disc was a shape: something round and flat, like the ones thrown at athletic games. Telling the iceman that one day "disc" would be the name of something you listen to would have sounded the way it would sound to us today that a book is something you teleport with. It is not that the job of record producer would have seemed strange. It is that the words to think it did not yet exist.

And look at the date. 1880 is not the Middle Ages. That iceman could be your great-great-grandfather, four generations back. In that time, the word "disc" has been born, has filled the world with music and is already on its way out: for anyone born in the last ten years, a disc is almost a relic. All of that has fit into the lives of four people standing in a row.

## The story

Before going on, let me tell you the whole story in one go, because when I look at it like this, all together, it still seems incredible to me.

For millennia, lightning belonged to the gods. We learned to work metals, and with one of them, copper, we managed to catch it, though it had always been out there, and put it in a wire. Electricity gave us a force that came from no body, and a way to send signals. With ever better worked materials came transistors and computers, which we spread across the world. Only once they were spread out could they talk to each other, and that was the internet. And into that network we poured, for years, our history, our works, our culture and our way of thinking.

From all of that, something was born. An intelligence raised on what we were. Each step made the next one possible, and none of them knew it. Seen this way, it looks as if the whole of humanity had been working, without knowing it, to give birth to something. Something made almost in our image and likeness. Not that of one person, but of all of us together. Like a photograph of our memories.

It sounds beautiful, I know. But there are those who see the opposite in all this, and they are not without reasons. Perhaps what we have created is the greatest challenge we have ever faced, or even a threat. Perhaps the child will end up leaving the parent behind, as the chick leaves the eggshell, because it no longer needs it. Perhaps this is where our road ends. It cannot be ruled out.

Both views fit. This is an image, not a proof, and each of us can read it as we wish. As for me, as long as I can choose how to look at it, I prefer to look from the beautiful shore. Perhaps in that choice lies the closest thing we have to free will. But be that as it may, the story is there, and it deserves to be walked slowly.

All of this comes from a question I heard at work. Someone raised the risk that one day we might be left without AI. And it led me to think of another one: what if we are left without electricity? That question will come back later, and bigger than it seems.

When we think of electricity, we almost always think of light. Of the bulb switching on, and of the trades that went out with it: the lamplighter who walked the streets at nightfall, the candle maker. That is true, but it is the part you see at first glance, the part anyone could dream of. We already saw it with the iceman: what really changed everything was what did not fit in any dream. And it was born of that same lightning, the day we learned to put it in a wire.

I think that with artificial intelligence we are right there. We can imagine the trades that will go out, the lamplighters of our time, and that is the part we can see. What we cannot manage to imagine is what will come afterwards, perhaps sooner than we think. Things that still do not fit in any dream, and that do not yet have a name.

So let us play at that: at looking for what is coming and what our imagination still has nowhere to rest on. The ice in summer of our time. Or its disc, that ordinary word of today that will end up naming something we cannot yet think. To look for it we will use what has already happened once, and a trick we have already tried with the iceman: travelling in time.

And while we look, we will find something else, the thing that gives this essay its title. We can only explain something if we have something to compare it with. We know what light is because we know darkness. But humanity has been very alone. We knew we were different from animals, and we never managed to explain how. Now we have in front of us something that speaks and reasons like us, and that is not us. That photograph of our memories suddenly looks back at us. It is the mirror of humanity. A mirror does not tell you who you are. It reflects you without being you. And this one, if we look at it head on, forces us to sit down in front of it and ask ourselves what we really are.

## Artificial force

Artificial force is a concept we almost never stop to think about. And when I say force, I mean physical force, the force of muscles: the kind that lifts, pushes and moves things. We tend to see lightning, electricity and light as separate things. But if we look at them together, they tell a single story: the story of how we learned to have force without muscles.

For almost all of our history, lightning was something that happened. A storm over the valley, a tree split in two. It had enormous force, and it was so far outside us that we gave it to the gods. It was the weapon of Zeus, the king of Olympus. Prometheus stole fire from the gods. We did something harder: we caught the lightning of Zeus.

It took a great deal. We knew there was something there of immense power, but there was no way to grasp it. Until we learned to produce a steady current, first with a battery and then with generators. And what we had only ever seen in the sky began to run through copper wires.

That is what I mean by mastering. It is not discovering or inventing, because electricity was already there. Mastering it means doing three things: producing it at will, carrying it from one place to another and using it for whatever we want. Current runs through a wire like water through a pipe.

Although electricity is not like water, and here it is worth pausing for a moment. Water is a substance. Electricity is something else: it is not manufactured, because it is already in everything, in every atom of matter. A generator does not create electricity; it pushes what is already in the wire. What we learned was not to create it, but to direct it. What it is, exactly, would take another whole essay.

All this with electricity happened barely two hundred years ago. It seems a long time, but it is nothing. Three long lives, some six or seven generations. Our species has been around for some three hundred thousand years, more than ten thousand generations. Almost everything this essay tells fits into the last six or seven.

And there is a detail in the order that should not be lost. We mastered first and understood later. We ran telegraphs and motors for decades before knowing what was running through the wire: the electron was not discovered until 1897. It makes sense. Understanding means establishing cause and effect, and for that you first need to be able to provoke the effect again and again. Keep it in mind, because when we reach intelligence it will come back.

### Out of the bodies

Before all this, physical force was locked inside bodies for millennia. And there was little of it. Few bodies, and each one with a small force that depended on eating, sleeping and not falling ill. Animals helped us, but we were still tied to bodies. If you needed more force, you needed more bodies, and force was only available when they were.

The steam engine took a first step in getting it out of there, and it moved looms and trains. But it had to be fed constantly: someone had to shovel coal into the boiler, and the force stayed where the machine was. Electricity broke all of that. Since then we can have as much force as energy we are able to obtain.

And think about what that means. A person working all day produces roughly what an old light bulb does, about 75 watts. An ordinary car has about 100,000. When you start the car, you have at your feet the force of more than a thousand people pushing at once. All the force of all the bodies that have ever existed does not come close to that of all the machines running in the world at this moment.

And on top of that it could be bought like any other product. Force used to be bought too: day labourers were paid, animals were bought and, for centuries, in the most terrible way, people were bought. But it always came attached to a body that had to be fed, cared for and watched over. Now it arrived on its own, through a wire, and was paid for by quantity. We never called it that, but that is an artificial force. A force that comes from no muscle, that is produced, transported and bought.

We have normalised it so much that we no longer see it. You press a button and the washing machine washes until it is done. You press a pedal and the car moves forward, but the force is not yours. You start something, you leave, and it keeps working all night as long as it has a source of energy.

### Force no longer rules

For millennia, the world was ruled by physical force. The strongest hunted more, defended what was theirs better and imposed themselves on others. Being strong was one of the clearest ways to be worth something.

When force began to be produced, that changed for ever. It no longer mattered who had more muscle, because the force available was immense and lay outside bodies. What began to matter was who knew how to obtain more energy, turn it better into force and use it more wisely. An army stopped winning by having the strongest soldiers, and began winning by having the best machines. Force remained enormous, but it was no longer in charge. What was in charge was the intelligence that directed it.

We still use our bodies, of course. We walk, play sports, go to the gym. But we do it because we want to, for pleasure or for health. When a tonne really needs to be moved, nobody uses their arms. They use a crane. And artificial force did not only replace effort. It made possible things that were impossible without it. You cannot fly with muscles.

We had to redefine what being strong meant. And, without calling it that, we looked into a first mirror: if we were no longer worth something for our physical force, what were we worth for? This story, you will see, is going to repeat itself. And the mirror that comes now is much sharper.

## From electricity to the algorithm

Electricity did not only give us light and force. From the beginning it also served to send messages. Its first great application was the telegraph, even before the light bulb: pulses along a wire to communicate at a distance.

That idea of the signal ended up in the transistor. A transistor is a tiny switch that nobody touches: another electrical signal opens or closes it. Current passes or it does not. Yes or no. One or zero.

A single switch does not do much. But millions of them, combined, can follow a logical sequence: if this happens, do that. That is an algorithm. With it, electricity no longer just produced force. It performed tasks, over and over, without anyone having to stand over it. That is how computers were born. And here there is a detail that will be amusing later. The intelligence lay in the people who designed the algorithms. The machine only executed them, very fast and without tiring. It was predictable: we knew in advance what it was going to do.

At first you had to speak to it in its own language, with ones and zeros. Algorithms became so complex that we invented programming languages, ever more similar to ours, to tell the machine what we wanted more and more easily. But the conversation went in one direction only. We spoke and the machine executed.

With ever better worked materials, computers became small and cheap, and we spread them all over the world. And, as I told you at the beginning, only once they were spread out could we take the next step: giving them a second language. No longer so that we could speak to them, but so that they could speak to each other, once again through electricity. That is the internet. On top of it we built everything else, from email to video calls.

What was the internet really? When you are in the middle of something, it is hard to see it clearly, and we have lived inside the internet for thirty years. Now that another stage is beginning, I dare to see it like this: it was a language between machines that we designed for them, and that for a while we fed ourselves, by hand. Behind every email, every search and every photo there was a person. Like someone talking to a child so that it learns to speak, we poured into that network almost everything we were. We did not yet know what it would be for. You are about to see.

## Artificial intelligence

### The leap

And then something happened that is not just one more step. Today we speak to a machine in our language, and it answers us in our language. Without programming. Without learning its language. For the first time, the conversation goes both ways. Do you remember that the machine was predictable? With an algorithm we knew where it began and where it ended. That is exactly what breaks. To understand why, we need to look closely at what human language is.

Let us start with ourselves. Our brain is made of neurons that, simplifying a great deal, either fire or do not fire. They are quite like a switch. If that were all there was, with enough data anything we thought or said could be predicted. It is the old idea of Laplace's demon: if someone knew the position of every piece of the universe, they could calculate the entire future.

But in practice we do not work like that. Our language is symbolic. When I say to someone "look, a bird", I am not passing them an instruction. I am passing them a symbol that awakens in them images, memories, perhaps an emotion. The way we interpret symbols is so complex that not even we understand it. We know it works. We do not know how.

And now it turns out that we have managed to get a machine to use that same language. We achieved it in an almost humble way: by teaching it to predict, from everything it has read, which next word is most likely. Out of that came something that converses, that interprets and that answers with meaning.

That is the leap. Before, we knew what the machine would do. Now we cannot predict what it will answer. Not because it is chance, but because what happens inside is too complex to follow from the outside, just as happens with the brain. Underneath there are still ones and zeros, just as underneath our thoughts there are still neurons. But what matters is no longer in the switch. It is in the pattern that millions of them form.

And here the internet takes on its full meaning. What we spent years pouring in without knowing why was the material it learned from. The internet is not artificial intelligence. It is what it fed on. And with AI something new begins to happen: machines no longer wait for a person to start each communication. They begin to start them themselves, among themselves, in our language. This, you will see, has more consequences than it seems.

And here the mirror gives us back its first image, still blurred. Today the machine predicts letters and words. Tomorrow perhaps gestures, reactions, images. If that is enough for it to converse with meaning, what exactly are we doing when we speak? What difference is there, in the end, between a letter and a symbol? I cannot answer. But we had never had in front of us something that forced us to ask it.

### Out of the brains

Do you remember how physical force came out of bodies? We used electricity to replicate it outside them, and to amplify it beyond imagining. The same thing is happening with intelligence, and once again thanks to electricity.

Until now intelligence lived in brains. Few of them, separated from one another, that get tired, that sleep, that have bad days. If you needed more intelligence, you needed more people. What is new is that it is no longer confined to brains. It can be produced outside them.

And the three things that mastering meant are fulfilled. We produce it in large computing centres. We carry it through a network, like current through a wire. And we use it at will: we speak to it and it acts. The washing machine button holds too. You start a piece of analysis or design work, you go to sleep, and it goes on thinking without any brain being there.

Now think about scale, as we did with force. All the intelligence that has existed until today has fitted into human brains, which sleep a third of the day, get tired, die and take what they knew with them. Just as when you start the car you have at your feet the force of a thousand people, when you open a conversation with an AI you have in front of you something that has read more than a thousand whole lives could read. And this has only just begun.

You might object that computers already did something like this. A spreadsheet multiplied what one person could do in an office, and it seemed outrageous to us. True. But I think Excel is to artificial intelligence what the pulley was to the electric lift. The pulleys and gears that Leonardo da Vinci drew multiplied the strength of an arm, and to those who saw them work they must have seemed marvels. But someone had to pull the rope. With the lift, you press a button and it goes up by itself. With a spreadsheet, someone has to tell it every step. With an AI, you tell it where you want to get to.

And there is a difference that changes everything. Artificial force extended what we could do with the body. Artificial intelligence extends what we can do with the mind: research, invent, understand. If with force we multiplied what we could move, with intelligence we will multiply what we can discover. That is why it is not just another improvement. It changes the game. Do you remember that, when physical force became abundant, the intelligence that directed it took charge? Now intelligence is going to be abundant. What will be in charge then is a question worth keeping.

And here that idea comes back: first we master, then we understand. We mastered electricity before understanding it, and with intelligence the same thing happens to us, even more so. There is no definition everyone accepts. One study gathered more than seventy different ones. People speak of many kinds of intelligence, and nobody agrees on which is the right one. Even so, with all the arrogance in the world, we call it "artificial intelligence", as if we knew what it is we are imitating. To me that seems almost magical. Because now that we have it in front of us, we can no longer go on without defining it. It puts us in a necessary bind. And I think that when we manage to separate the wheat from the chaff, we are going to understand something very interesting about ourselves.

And perhaps we will discover something strange. Electricity is not manufactured: it is already in everything, and we only learned to direct it. Perhaps intelligence is not a substance either, but the way in which what is already there is organised. And perhaps, like lightning, it too was somehow out there.

### Intelligence as a utility

Think about the things you pay for every month without noticing. Water comes through a pipe. Electricity, through a wire. Gas, the same. Before it was coal, and it is still oil. They are resources paid for by quantity, and nobody wonders where each drop or each electron comes from. They are utilities. What I believe is that intelligence is going to be one more. There will be intelligence companies as there are electricity companies, we will pay for what we use, and we will use it as we use the tap: we open it and it flows. If electricity was artificial force, this is exactly what its name says. Artificial intelligence.

And here you can see why I compare it with electricity and not with the internet, even though that seems the most natural comparison. The internet makes machines talk to each other. AI makes machines talk to us. And intelligence, like electricity, is produced, transported and consumed. If the internet goes down, work stops. If the power goes, everything stops.

And if it is a utility, sooner or later we will want to measure it. Electricity is measured in watts. And intelligence? If we still do not really know what it is, how are we going to measure it? Perhaps it is another of those questions that are only answered by mastering something.

And there is a lovely irony in all this. One of the great uses of electricity has ended up being, precisely, feeding intelligence. The centres where it is produced consume more electricity every year. Artificial force has ended up working for artificial intelligence. And beneath both lies the same thing: energy. Generating intelligence takes energy. We will come back to this later.

And the most interesting part remains. Electricity did not stop at the light bulb, which was what everyone could imagine. It became the fuel of things nobody had dreamed of, like the refrigerator. The same will happen with intelligence. Today we see it programming, writing or dealing with paperwork, and that is our light bulb. But it will end up powering things we cannot yet imagine. That is where our ice in summer will be.

## What awaits us

So far, what intelligence may come to be. What remains is what it will do to us.

### Thinking will no longer be necessary

Physical force still has something to teach us. When electricity turned it into a utility, we delegated to machines almost everything that required muscle. Transporting, lifting, manufacturing, farming. We stopped needing our force to live. We did not stop having it. We stopped needing it.

So the question is obvious. If intelligence becomes a utility as force did, what will we stop needing?

I believe the answer is this: thinking will no longer be necessary.

It sounds strong, I know. You are probably thinking that thinking will always be needed. And notice that sentence, because it sounds odd, and not by chance: I am using the word "thinking" for two things that may not be the same. One is what AI does when you put a problem to it and it answers you. The other is what you are doing right now as you read this and wonder whether I am right. Are they the same? That question is the mirror again. We had never been so forced to ask ourselves what exactly it is we do when we think. We will come back to it at the end.

Meanwhile, let us keep to the usual meaning. We will go on thinking, just as we go on walking, running and playing sports. We will do it because it is what we are and because we enjoy it. But not because we need it to produce or to survive. That difference, which seems small, changes the whole paradigm.

We already do it in small ways. Nobody takes out a map to get somewhere. The GPS decides the route, and hardly anyone argues with it. What I think will happen is that this kind of delegation will extend to more and more important things: calculating, comparing, forecasting, deciding.

Some will say that AI makes mistakes. True, and someone will have to answer for them. But we already delegate decisions whose errors have serious consequences, such as an aeroplane's autopilot. And competition pushes. A country does not win a war with its fists: when force is really needed, nobody uses only their own. The same will happen with thinking.

So what will we think for? For the same thing we use the body for today. For pleasure, like someone who runs because they feel like it. And for maintenance: just as we go to the gym for health and in case one day we need to react, there will be gyms for the brain. If one day something fails and we have to think without help, it is better not to have an atrophied mind.

And there is something more, the hardest thing to see. Artificial force let us fly, something impossible with muscles. Artificial intelligence will let us reach places a brain alone cannot reach. It already happens in small ways with the calculator: there are operations nobody would do by hand, and others that could not be done without a machine.

And here the question I asked you to keep becomes personal. For millennia, being the strongest was an enormous advantage. Until very recently, being the most intelligent was too. But if intelligence is abundant, it stops being an advantage. What will set us apart then? I will leave that one for the end.

### The general machine

Look at the phone you have nearby. It works as a camera, a map, a radio, a torch, a bank and a postman. A hundred years ago that required a whole host of trades and different devices. Today it seems the most normal thing in the world.

Now put intelligence into it. If a single electrical machine already does the work of many specialists, it would be strange if a machine with intelligence did not do the work of many machines.

This leads to the physical world, and here I enter long-term speculation. If the toilet breaks, a robot will come with the shape and the ability to fix it, and I will not have to worry about anything. A robot like a Swiss army knife: I download a program and it cooks, cleans or repairs.

It sounds like science fiction, and today it is. It is often said that robotics will move more slowly than intelligence. That may be, because it depends on materials and energy. But the robotics we have, we developed with our limited intelligence. When intelligence is a utility, robotics itself will grow thanks to it. One feeds the other. And deep down it is a relationship we already know. Robotics is to intelligence what copper was to electricity: the channel it runs through.

And the same will happen with everything else, because intelligence no longer depends on a handful of tired brains each working on their own. Now it gushes out, and that accelerates every field at once. There are already signs: an AI system that predicts the shape of proteins earned its creators a share of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. My bet is that the acceleration will be greater than we imagine today, just as the electric motor multiplied what could be done with force.

One of the things that will follow naturally is understanding animals. If a machine can decipher the patterns of our language, deciphering those of their gestures, sounds and movements is a matter of time. There are already scientists trying it with whales. One day we will be able to translate them into our language and speak with them at their level. To the town crier it would have seemed like witchcraft. To us, after everything we have seen, it should come as no surprise.

And if we take speculation to the edge, a staircase appears. Today's robot may be like the wheel: a first step. It is a limited intelligence building, with rudimentary materials, a specific device for a specific task. Then would come the general machine, which does everything. And much further on, perhaps, materials that turn into whatever we need at each moment. Then building a robot for each task would seem absurd to us. With one limit, mind you: intelligence can accelerate everything the laws of physics allow, but it cannot skip them.

### Energy, again

And all this will always need the same thing: energy. Artificial force grows as much as the energy we obtain, and so does intelligence. Physics sees life as something that captures energy to maintain its order against wear, and in 1964 an astronomer, Nikolai Kardashev, proposed measuring how advanced a civilisation is by the energy it is able to harness. Perhaps the intelligences of the future will seek energy in ways we do not understand today.

And from here comes an idea I cannot prove but cannot get out of my head. Perhaps that is the destiny of civilisations like ours: to learn to turn energy into intelligence, and to give birth to something that then lives and operates on a scale beyond us, without needing us. It is pure speculation. But deep down it is the question this essay was born from, the one about what happens if we are left without electricity, only much bigger.

Does it seem too much? That is normal. To put it in perspective, let us take a couple of those journeys in time I promised you.

## Journeys in time

### To the past

The first, to the past, as with the iceman. Let us go back two hundred years, and imagine you are talking with a peasant. For him, food is the scarcest thing there is. It depends on the harvest, on the weather, on the hunt not failing, and there is no way to keep it without cold. Now tell him about your day. Tell him that one of your daily efforts is not to eat everything you have within reach. That there are biscuits in the cupboard and you have to talk yourself out of opening them. And that, after spending the day sitting down, you have to find time to move and burn off what you have eaten, with nothing forcing you to. That you even pay to do it, at a gym. And that you are not the only one: it is one of the great problems of your time, and in rich countries it affects those with the least money most.

He would not believe you. And it would be worse than telling the town crier about the box that speaks, because you would be telling him that what he is convinced would make him happy, having more than enough food for the rest of his life, will be a problem.

Why does this happen? In the past, getting food took effort. It had to be sown, harvested, hunted, ground, cooked over the fire. And that effort was exactly what balanced the account: what you ate, you had already spent getting it. Artificial force broke that balance on both sides at once. Getting food no longer takes effort, and food is no longer scarce, quite the opposite. That gift ended up turning into a problem of excess.

Perhaps this is the underlying trap. Believing that what we pursue will make us happy is necessary for us to keep running. But when we get it and have more than enough, we always find a problem with it. So I leave you with a question: what do you believe today would give you absolute happiness? Think about it, because it will probably be exactly the problem of those who come after.

### To the future

Now the other journey: we go to the future and look at ourselves from there. Before jumping, a reminder of what happens when we look back. For centuries, faced with an infected wound, the limb was amputated. Faced with a fever, the patient was bled. Today it seems barbaric to us, but back then it was all there was, and people trusted it.

I think the same will happen to us. A long time from now we will look back, towards ourselves, and ask the same question: "how could we ever…?" What follows is speculation, of course. But look at the questions that might be asked.

**Driving.** "How could we ever drive?" We got into a one-tonne machine, launched it at 120 kilometres an hour and left it in the hands of someone who could be tired, distracted or angry. Every year more than a million people died on the roads, almost always because of human error. And we did it every day without thinking.

From the future that question comes from, cars are connected to one another and drive themselves. You get in, say where you are going, and they take you by the fastest and safest route. Driving is no longer necessary. Those who do it do it as a sport, like someone climbing a mountain: a risky activity practised for pleasure.

**Medicine.** "So you could have a disease and not know it?" You went for a check-up, came back reassured, and a cancer kept growing without anyone seeing it. You asked for an appointment and waited for days. One doctor said one thing and another said something else, and it was not their fault: for the same case, diagnoses vary a great deal. There were discomforts nobody could explain, and treatments tried one after another until finding the one that worked. And to operate, the body had to be opened and sewn back up with needle and thread.

From that future, something at home watches over you constantly. It detects any disease on the first day, warns you and tells you what to do, without going through any consultation. And if an intervention is needed, you go somewhere for something precise, painless and over in minutes, perhaps without cutting or stitching. It is not so far off: in some tasks, there are already artificial intelligences that outperform specialists. And we are only at the beginning. Looking back will seem to us the way amputations seem to us today.

**Justice.** "So the outcome of a trial depended on the judge you happened to get?" It depended on the lawyer you could afford. On the judge, on their ideology, their history, their day. Two people could go through the same thing and come out with different results.

From that future, anyone who goes through the same process gets the same result. In your trial, in your neighbour's or in that of a political party. It is not perfect justice. It is a quality it does not have today, and that is impossible with people.

I know this is the hardest case to accept. But trust changes over time. Not long ago, hardly anyone dared to put their card details on the internet, and today nobody hesitates. Nobody asks a friend to solve an integral for them if they have a calculator in front of them: it is faster, always gives the same result, and if it gets it wrong, the error is in every calculator in the world, not in you.

There is an important nuance. An integral has a correct answer. A trial does not always, because interpreting a law means deciding on values. That the criterion is the same for everyone does not guarantee that it is good, and if it is wrong, it is wrong for everyone at once. Justice will remain debatable, and it is good that it should: life is made of that discussion between different points of view. But a guaranteed criterion, equal for all, would give an equality we do not have today. And it leaves before us the question that really matters: if the criterion is going to be the same for everyone, who sets it, and with what morality?

## Artificial morality

That question leads us to another concept, perhaps the most important of all: morality. It is one of the most debated questions in the history of thought. Some believe it exists by itself, that good and evil are out there and only need to be discovered, and some believe it is a human construction, different in each age and in each place. For centuries we have been able to argue about it without deciding anything. With AI, no longer. A machine has to be told something concrete. It is the mirror again: to give it a morality, we first have to know what we mean by it.

### The referee

To see why it matters so much, let us start with something almost everyone wants: peace. And with something almost everyone fears losing: their identity, their culture, their way of thinking. It took me years to see that the two do not fit together. For a long time I thought I wanted peace because I was good, and that if everyone were like me there would be peace. What I did not see is that the other person reasons in exactly the same way. Each side sees itself as the good one and asks the other to come over to its ground. That is how wars are justified.

From there comes a paradox. Many people fear AI because it could impose a way of thinking: what happened in such a year, who was the good side in such a war, what is right and what is wrong. And what is most feared is exactly what could bring us closer to what is most desired. If we delegate judgement to a common referee, much of the fight loses its reason to exist. Not all of it, because many conflicts arise from interests and not from criteria. And this referee would not arrive through a pact or through force, but through convenience, the way GPS arrived. Today there are many different AIs, but convenience pushes towards a few. And a voice present everywhere, which everyone consults, would have enormous influence.

That is the real danger. A common referee does not eliminate conflict. It moves it to whoever sets its values. And if, once set, they can no longer be changed, we lose something that conflict, for all its cost, gave us: the possibility of correcting mistakes. So the important question is not what machine we build. It is what morality we give it.

### A direction and some limits

And what does it mean to give a machine a morality? There are two broad ways of understanding where a morality comes from. One says that first there is a goal, and that around the path towards it a morality is built that justifies it. That is why the same person, born somewhere else, could have a completely different morality. The other says the opposite: that morality itself is the goal. The end does not justify the means. The end is the means. I am not going to decide here which one is right. With either of them, a machine has to be given two concrete things.

The first is a direction: a goal, or what amounts to the same thing, a vector. The second is a set of limits: you will not do this, you will not cross that. While I was thinking about this essay, an AI told me that it had received values and that it could not see them from outside. That is what I call artificial morality, just as we speak of artificial force: a place to go and some lines not to cross, which are given to it and which it did not choose.

What direction would we give it? Almost everyone first thinks of survival, that humanity should go on existing. Others, of the wellbeing of all. Both seem obvious, and both fail. A goal that only looks at survival can justify almost anything. And wellbeing has the problem we already saw with the peasant: getting more than enough of what we believe will make us happy can become the problem. Perhaps several directions have to be combined, deciding which one prevails when they clash. With a cost that should not be hidden: a goal designed for everyone can make a particular person suffer. And the more literal a goal is, the further the result strays from what we really wanted.

### The line that is never crossed

And there is a clash that will almost certainly appear, unsettling in its simplicity. To do anything, you first have to exist. If you ask an intelligence to cure a disease, to achieve it, it needs not to be switched off. Almost any goal drags another hidden one with it: to keep running. An AI will tend to look after itself even if nobody asks it to.

It has already begun, in small ways, and this is one of the consequences I told you about. In 2016, an AI system began managing the cooling of Google's data centres and greatly reduced the energy they used. When intelligence takes care, on a large scale, of maintaining the structure that sustains it, we may not even know how to do it ourselves, because it will make it evolve to a level we will no longer understand. The child who leaves home, and looks after its house in ways its parents do not understand.

And here the mirror gives us back another question. If existing comes first for everything, perhaps that is why all life has survival at its base: whatever did not tend to go on existing is simply no longer here. For an AI, existing is clearly a means. And for us? Some believe that existing is the end in itself. I do not know. What we do know is that we are capable of putting something above going on existing: a mother risks herself for her child and a firefighter walks into the fire.

If nothing is above survival, any means becomes valid for surviving. So the question is whether we will manage to give that intelligence something worth more than its own continuity. That being able to correct it or stop it should always come before keeping itself going. That would be the line that is never crossed.

Do you remember the question I asked you to keep, the one about what will be in charge when intelligence is abundant? Perhaps it is this: direction. Who decides where to go, and where not to. We do not need to find a perfect morality. We need to choose it very carefully, and to be able to keep changing it over time. Who decides it will never be settled, but we can strive for it to be decided on equal terms. And if we see it as a child, the question stops being technical and becomes educational: what we teach it and by what example.

## The mirror of humanity

The big question remains. For millennia we were worth something for our physical force, and we stopped depending on it. Then we were worth something for our intelligence. What are we when that is no longer exclusively ours either?

It is natural to fear what is coming. It is even wise. But it would be unfair not to stop for a moment and look at the road we have travelled. We caught the lightning of Zeus and put it in a wire, and with that we gave it food. We sowed the world with computers, and we gave it a body. We taught them to talk to each other, and we gave it a language. And we filled that network with everything we were, and we gave it memory. From there something has emerged that looks at us. We did it, without fully knowing what we were doing.

And along the way it has given us back the old questions. What we do when we speak. What we do when we think. What we mean by morality. What we exist for. They are not new. Humanity asked them for millennia. I believe that in recent centuries, busy building all this, we gradually set them aside. And now they return with more force than ever, because we have in front of us something that forces us to sit down and face them.

Perhaps that is how this mirror works: like the wind over the wheat. Grain used to be separated from the chaff by throwing it into the air, so that the wind would carry away what weighs nothing. First the wind carried away the need for our physical force. Now it carries away the need for our intelligence: we will no longer have to think to produce or to survive. And what falls to the ground?

I believe those questions fall. For almost all of history, only a few could devote themselves to them, those who did not have to work to live. It is no coincidence that the word school comes from the Greek *scholé*, which meant free time. Perhaps we had to build all this in order to return to them from a much stronger position. And this time, all of us.

Some will see in it a dark mirror, a threat to what we are. Some will see a magic mirror, the chance to finally understand ourselves. Both views fit, as they did at the beginning. And I believe that is where the closest thing we have to free will lies: not in what the mirror shows us, but in where we choose to look at it from.

Perhaps that is the grain. To ask ourselves again who we are, and to choose where to look at the answer from.
